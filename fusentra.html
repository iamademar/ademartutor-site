02What we built

The right intervention, at the right moment, for the right household.

Rewards card data tells us which households are at risk and when. We don't guess that "this item is old." We classify each household into one of six food-waste segments, based on why they waste — then time and word the nudge to match. A household that overbuys perishables gets a different intervention from one that over-trusts best-before dates.

The segment taxonomy comes from a real study of loyalty-card grocery data: households were surveyed on why their food gets wasted, then a decision tree was trained to predict those self-reported reasons from purchase history alone.

Segments and classifier built on the labels and datasets from Närvänen et al. (2023), International Journal of Consumer Studies 47(4), 1273–1290 (DOI 10.1111/ijcs.12903). Purchase-history feature families (shopping frequency, planning regularity, overprovisioning, discount sensitivity): Merian et al., 2022, Sustainability (DOI 10.3390/su141610183).

We built a decision pipeline for choosing the right MCP tools. Two things get decided, and it's worth separating them. The classifier decides how a household is spoken to — the framing, the wording, which tip gets pulled from the corpus. What the household is actually asked to do comes from somewhere else entirely: how much of the item's shelf life has already elapsed. A household can be an overpurchaser and still get a compost prompt, if the spinach is simply too far gone to eat or share.

Some segments read cleanly from purchase behaviour — overpurchaser/overpreparer households, who buy in bulk and let perishables turn, score 0.91 F1. Others, like households who over-trust date labels, are attitudinal rather than behavioural: the underlying research says purchase data alone can't fully capture them, and a real deployment would inherit that limit. 03How it works · The recovery hierarchy

Eat it, share it, compost it — in that order, on purpose.

This ordering isn't our invention. Prevent, Share, Divert is a simplified adaptation of the food-waste hierarchy promoted by Love Food Hate Waste NZ: prevent waste first, feed people second, and only divert to compost or landfill once both of those have failed.

The reason for the order is value, not tidiness. Eating the food recovers its full nutritional and economic value. Sharing it recovers most of that value for someone else. Composting only recovers the soil nutrients — everything else is already lost by that point. So the system escalates in that sequence, and never skips a step.

While shelf life remains Eat it

Recipe nudge, timed to what's about to turn.

Unopened / whole produce only Share it

Routed to a real, named pātaka kai nearby that accepts it.

Last resort Compost it

Council collection info, only once the first two have failed.

Sharing is deliberately narrow: only unopened, whole items — like uncut produce or shelf-stable packaged goods — are ever routed to a pātaka kai. Cut or opened perishables, dairy, meat and fish, and prepared meals are excluded outright, because the hierarchy only works if the "share it" step never puts anyone's safety at risk. That's a recovery hierarchy, not a reminder app.

The MCP stack · 01The supply side

We turn nonprofit websites into MCP tools.

Every tool in the diagrams above has to come from somewhere. The tips, the pātaka kai directory, the council's organics service — each one is expertise a non-profit already publishes on its own website, and none of them are going to build an AI integration to expose it.

So we built the thing that does it for them. MCPublish lets an organisation register a URL, pick which sections of its site to expose, and publishes them as tools any AI assistant can call — crawled, indexed, and answered from the organisation's own pages rather than from a model's memory of them. No app to build, no data handed over, no content scraped into someone else's training set.

This is the supply side of the stack the rest of this pitch consumes. The Love Food Hate Waste and pātaka kai servers behind the demo stand in for exactly what a partner would publish this way.

04Evidence · AI agent simulation

Twelve household types. Twelve weeks. A real drop.

We simulated the full loop end to end — half the households got nudges starting week five, half didn't.

Each household is a simulated persona given a profile and a weekly situation, and asked to make one decision. Twelve household types, run for twelve simulated weeks, produce the outcomes below. Every one was simulated twice — once getting nudges, once not — across three seeds, giving 36 treatment runs against 36 control runs. Each household is its own control, which removes between-household variance.

No real households were involved. The personas are AI agents (gpt-5.5), with New Zealand suburbs and grocery prices.

Method: our food-waste simulation adapts the persona-aligned agent design introduced in PAARS: Persona Aligned Agentic Retail Shoppers (Mansour et al., 2025), in which LLM agents are conditioned on synthetic personas derived from historical purchase data. We extend this approach beyond shopping behaviour to simulate household food consumption and disposal decisions.

Compliance rates are calibrated from published literature, not observed — this proves the loop works end to end, ready to retrain the moment real data exists.

The MCP stack · 03The pattern

We built a platform for enterprise AI to access and use MCP tools.

Look again at the three partners behind that demo. Love Food Hate Waste's tips. The pātaka kai directory. The council's organics service. Each one is a tool the organisation hosts and owns — that a corporation's AI can call, but never copy, scoped in code so it can only be used the way its owner intended.

Non-profits shouldn't have to build apps. They should publish their expertise as tools.

The plumbing is the Model Context Protocol — the open standard the AI industry has already adopted for connecting models to tools. We didn't invent a protocol; we built what the community sector plugs into it: a set of tool contracts where the non-profit keeps its data on its own server, under its own governance, and every answer comes back attributed to the source. Nothing is centralised. Nothing is scraped into a model. That's data sovereignty by architecture, not by policy document.

And the corporate half of this is already built. Woolworths runs Olive today as an agentic assistant — one that calls tools to plan meals and build shopping lists. Every bank, telco and airline is shipping the same thing. Agentic assistants are arriving either way. What doesn't exist yet is the community-sector half: the tools those agents can safely call, and the scoping that governs every call. That's the half Fusentra governs, and the food-waste app demonstrates.

And the caller doesn't get free rein. Fusentra's deterministic state machine decides the tier — PREVENT, SHARE, or DIVERT — before any partner is contacted, and each decision is only allowed to reach the one partner server scoped to that tier:

That scoping is enforced in code, not just described in a diagram — a SHARE decision physically cannot reach the LFHW server, and a PREVENT decision cannot reach the council server. An out-of-tier call raises PermissionError, and 27 automated tests confirm the scoping holds and that a nudge built from an MCP call passes the exact same validation as one built from local data.

05The engine

Everything you just watched is a plugin. Three ways in. One governed engine.

The food-waste app doesn't own any of the machinery it uses. It supplies three things — a set of classifiers, a purchase log, and a set of tools — and Fusentra does the rest. Swap those three and the engine runs somebody else's workflow without a line of it changing.

Way in · 01 Classifier plugins

Six household segments and a shelf-life fraction, here. Your own classifiers, anywhere else.

Way in · 02 Your purchase log

The source of truth every claim is validated against before it can be sent.

Way in · 03 Tools plugin

Love Food Hate Waste, the pātaka kai network — or any MCP tools you generate.

What sits between them is a single governed decision pipeline. Every workflow runs the same four steps, in the same order, every time:

01 Classify

Is this worth acting on, and who are we speaking to?

02 Enforce tier

Which tools this decision may reach — and no others.

03 Validate

Every claim checked against the source data before it leaves.

04 Log

Every decision recorded, auditable after the fact.

The assistant connects in over MCP at the far end — Olive, in this case, but the engine doesn't care which assistant it is. Open source core. And it stays open: your own classifiers, the governance layer (tier enforcement, audit log), and the interfaces (MCP server, plugin API).
